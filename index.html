How Physicists Solve Multi-Stage Motion Problems

01

Segment by Acceleration Rule

You cannot use one formula across the whole story if acceleration changes! Cut the timeline into stages wherever $a$ changes — and only there. If $a$ stays the same, it is one interval, even if the object stops and turns around.

02

The "Baton Pass" Rule

Two things are handed off at every stage boundary. Velocity can't jump, so $v_{f,1} = v_{0,2}$. Position can't jump either, so $x_{f,1} = x_{0,2}$. Never assume $v_0 = 0$ or $x_0 = 0$ for later stages!

03

Local $\Delta t$ vs. Master Clock $t$

Kinematic formulas use duration $\Delta t_n$ within each stage. To get the overall trip time, add stage durations: $t_{\text{total}} = \Delta t_1 + \Delta t_2 + \Delta t_3$.

04

Total Displacement from $v$–$t$ Area

Each stage's area under the $v$–$t$ graph is its displacement: triangles give $\frac{1}{2}bh$, rectangles give $bh$, and area below the axis counts as negative. Total $\Delta x = \Delta x_1 + \Delta x_2 + \Delta x_3$. Distance traveled adds the areas without signs.

05

Position vs. Displacement

$x = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 + v_0t + x_0$ tells you where the object is ($t$ = time since the stage began). $\Delta x = v_0\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a(\Delta t)^2$ tells you how far it moved during the stage. They are linked by $x = x_0 + \Delta x$ and agree only when $x_0 = 0$.
